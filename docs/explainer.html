Your bank statement is already public

Every Bitcoin payment ever made is written on a noticeboard that anyone can read, forever. Here is why that is a problem, and a trick that fixes it.

The problem

Whoever you pay can look you up

Bitcoin has no names, only addresses — long strings of letters and numbers. People assume that makes it anonymous. It does not. It makes it pseudonymous, which is a very different thing: the entire history is public, and the only question is whether anyone can connect an address to you.

Usually they can. Pay a shop once and that shop now has an address it knows is yours. It can look up how much you have left. What you were paid last month. Where you spend. So can your landlord, your employer, and anyone who has ever sent you money.

How they connect the dots

One rule does most of the work

Companies that trace Bitcoin need to bundle scattered addresses into "this is one person's wallet." They mostly do it with a single assumption:

If one payment spends two coins at once, the same person must have owned both of them.

Think of paying in cash. If you hand over two notes, obviously both notes were yours — you had them in your pocket. The same logic applies on Bitcoin, and it is right nearly every time. It is cheap to check across every payment ever made, and it is the foundation almost all Bitcoin surveillance is built on.

The trick

Let the shop chip in

There is a technique called payjoin. When you pay, the shop quietly adds one of its own coins to your payment before it goes out.

Back to cash: you hand over two notes, but one of them was slipped in by the shopkeeper. Anyone watching counts two notes and assumes both were yours. They are wrong — and nothing about the payment reveals that they are wrong.

The amounts stop making sense too. Because the shop's own coin goes in and comes straight back out, the number you actually paid never appears anywhere.

A real payment we made

What a snooper sees, and what happened

This is not a mock-up. We ran this payment for real and it is recorded publicly, like every other Bitcoin payment.

What a snooper concludes 130,000 was paid
  • One person made this payment
  • Both coins came from that person's wallet
  • These two wallets belong to the same someone — merge them and everything either has ever touched
What actually happened 30,000 was paid
  • Two different people, who had never dealt with each other before
  • One coin from each of them
  • The wallets belong to strangers — the snooper's link is false, and everything built on it is now wrong too

That last point is the part people miss. Payjoin does not just hide one payment. It feeds bad information into the tracing companies' records, and every conclusion they draw through that false link inherits the mistake.

The catch

So why doesn't everyone do this?

Because it takes two. A normal payment you can send on your own — the other person can be asleep, or offline for a year. Payjoin needs the shop to actually take part, because it has to add its coin and approve the result.

First try The shop had to be running a server, awake, at the exact second you paid. Fine for a big company. Impossible for a person with a phone in their pocket.
Second try Put a middleman company in between to hold the message until the shop wakes up. This works — but now a company sits in the middle of a privacy tool, and only a handful of them exist. Whoever runs one can be pressured, or simply switch it off.
Ours Use message boards that already exist, run by hundreds of unrelated people, and leave a sealed note. Nobody new has to run anything.
What we built

Leave a sealed note on a public board

There is an open network called nostr — thousands of simple message relays, run by ordinary people all over the world, already online. Anyone can post a message, and anyone can pick one up later.

So the shop publishes a payment request with a one-time name attached. You leave your half of the payment on those relays, sealed, addressed to that name. The relays cannot open it — to them it is a locked box with no return address. The shop collects it whenever it next comes online, and posts its reply the same way.

Being honest about the limit: the relays cannot read anything you send, but they do see the internet address you connected from — the same way any website does. Routing through Tor would close that, and we have not made it the default yet.

Does it actually work?

Yes, and the shop was switched off

We ran it. The receiving side printed its payment request and then we shut the program down entirely. The payer sent anyway, and the relays held the sealed note.

Some time later we started the receiver again. It picked up the note that had arrived while it was off, added its coin, and sent its half back. The payment went through — and it is now recorded publicly as the confusing, unreadable thing you saw above.
